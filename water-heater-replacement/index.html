How do you know it is time to replace a water heater rather than repair it? Start with the age. Tanks tend to wear out between eight and twelve years, and the serial number on the label dates yours precisely. Then look at the symptoms. A leak from the tank itself, rusty water from the hot side only, and loud sediment noise all point to a tank near the end. Parts like thermocouples, elements, and relief valves can be replaced on almost any heater, but a rusted-through tank cannot, and homeowners in Warwick, RI tend to learn that the hard way, with a wet floor.

A new water heater should fit the way your household uses hot water today, not the way it did when the old one went in. That is why Warwick Water Heater asks a few questions first: how many people shower in the morning, whether you fill a large tub, and how many bathrooms draw at once. For tank heaters we compare first-hour ratings; for tankless, we size to the gallons per minute you need at the temperature rise your incoming water demands in the coldest months. Heat pump models get a check for space and airflow, since they pull heat from the room around them. We also confirm your gas line, vent, and electrical panel can handle the new unit, so there are no surprises on installation day.

The swap itself follows a checklist, not a guess: the old tank drained and hauled away, new connectors and a working shutoff installed, gas lines leak-tested and fitted with a sediment trap, venting sized and pitched to the manufacturer's spec, and the relief valve piped to a safe discharge point. If local code requires a thermal expansion tank, a drain pan, or seismic straps, they go in, and the permit is pulled whenever one is needed. We finish by filling and purging the tank, checking draft and carbon monoxide on gas models, setting the thermostat near 120 degrees, and showing you around the new heater. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Rusty or brown water from the hot side only
  • A tank at or past the eight-to-twelve-year mark
  • Energy bills rising while hot water gets harder to come by
  • Hot water running out faster every month
  • Loud rumbling or popping that flushing no longer fixes
  • Rust or corrosion on the tank, fittings, or vent connector
  • Repeated service calls on the same aging heater
  • Water seeping out from under the tank jacket

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Swapping a water heater looks simple until you count the connections. Gas units need a leak-free connection with a sediment trap, a correctly sized and pitched vent that sends carbon monoxide outdoors, and enough combustion air. An electric unit needs a circuit and breaker matched to the element wattage. Every tank needs a correctly rated relief valve with a discharge pipe routed to a safe spot, and many homes also need an expansion tank, a drain pan, or seismic straps to meet local code. Moving to tankless or a heat pump raises the stakes further, with larger gas lines, new vent materials, dedicated circuits, and condensate drains.

Professional service handles the full job, not just the tank. We size the heater by first-hour rating or flow rate, verify the fuel, venting, and electrical capacity, and add the expansion tank, drain pan, or straps your home needs. Every gas and water joint is leak-tested, gas units get a draft and carbon monoxide check, and the old heater is hauled away. The result is a heater that is safe, sized for your household, and ready for inspection if one is required. If your current tank still has good years left and just needs a part, you will hear it from us directly.

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Here are our recommendations:

  • Clean the air filter on a heat pump water heater every few months
  • If you chose tankless, flush it annually to clear scale from the heat exchanger
  • Inspect the anode rod every few years; replacing it can add years to the tank
  • Plan ahead: knowing the heater's age lets you replace it on your schedule, not after a leak
  • Avoid running the tank hotter than about 120 degrees unless there is a specific need
  • Start a flushing routine in the first year, before sediment hardens
  • Make sure an expansion tank is in place if your home has a closed plumbing system
